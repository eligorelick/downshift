# Evidence behind the defaults

Each default below is either a value used in controlled studies ("tested") or a reasoned starting point that no study has tested in this form ("starting point"). The full review, with all sources and effect sizes, is [evidence-review.md](evidence-review.md).

## What the research supports

- Slow breathing gives a small-to-medium reduction in self-reported stress ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)). In the largest blinded trial a placebo pace helped as much ([Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49279-8)).
- Short breaks reduce fatigue and raise energy; their effect on performance in demanding work is small and uncertain ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)).
- No trial links breathing prompts or any break schedule to better grades.

## Breathing

| Default | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 4 s in, 6 s out, no holds | Six breaths a minute has the largest evidence base ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)); slower is better down to about six ([Czub et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3496)) | Pace tested |
| 5 in, 5 out offered as equal | Careful tests find no reliable benefit from a longer exhale ([Meehan & Shaffer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-024-09637-2)) | Tested |
| Cyclic sighing and box as options only | Sighing rests on one small unreplicated trial ([Balban et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)); holds add no shown benefit ([Marchant et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-025-09688-z)) | Small trials |
| 5-minute sessions | Sessions under 5 minutes were linked to failed interventions; longer adds nothing ([Bentley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci13121612)) | Tested |
| 2-minute option | Small pilot studies only ([De Couck et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2019.02.011)) | Pilot-grade |
| "Slower, not deeper" and a first-run explanation | Beginners over-breathe ([Szulczewski 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218550)); instruction marks the programmes that worked (Bentley et al. 2023) | Association |
| Tension rating as the score, not heart rate | Average heart rate barely changes with slow breathing ([Shao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2)) | Design inference |

## Heart rate nudge

No study validates stress detection from heart rate alone. Every number in this section is a starting point.

| Default | Why |
|---|---|
| Asks "Feeling tense?"; never states it | Heart rate cannot separate stress from movement ([Brouwer et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6054929/)) |
| +15 bpm above seated baseline | Stress without movement adds roughly 4 to 21 bpm ([Bigalke et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401978/)); real exams added about 9 ([Pakhomov et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7094857/)) |
| Held for 5 minutes, judged on per-minute medians | Stress lasts minutes; standing up adds about 16 bpm and is over in 30 seconds ([Heidelbach et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13018822/)); per-second wrist readings are unreliable during posture changes ([Van Oost et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12568089/)) |
| A sharp jump restarts the count | Same sources: a jump of that shape is movement or sensor error |
| Study sessions only | That is when the person is sitting |
| At most one an hour, three a day, silent, at a pause in typing | Interruptions cost errors and stress ([Altmann et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23294345/); [Mark et al. 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)); body-triggered prompts did no better than random ones ([Schwerdtfeger et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12371293/)) |
| 130 bpm for 10 minutes shows a check-in, not an exercise | Borrowed from consumer high-heart-rate alerts, which require ten minutes of inactivity |

## Study breaks

| Default | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 25-minute block, 5-minute break | The only schedule in randomized student trials, with mixed results ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593); [Smits et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15070861)) | Tested |
| 8-minute break after blocks over 25 minutes | Longer breaks help more within ten minutes (Albulescu et al. 2022) | Starting point |
| 20 to 30 minute break after about two hours | School test data ([Sievertsen et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)) | Length from field data; two hours is a starting point |
| Move, breathe or rest; leave the phone | Exercise and relaxation breaks beat an unstructured one ([Blasche et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.2830)); a phone break restored less ([Kang & Kurtzberg 2019](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.8.2019.21)) | Tested for mood and fatigue |
| Two-minute warning, two delays, a resume note | Mid-task interruption is costly ([Adamczyk & Bailey 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985727)); noting the next step helps ([Leroy & Glomb 2018](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184)) | Starting point |

## Verification note

The figures from Pakhomov 2020 and Van Oost 2025 were re-checked against the papers during development. The remaining sources were read by the review's authors as noted in the full review, some from abstracts only.
