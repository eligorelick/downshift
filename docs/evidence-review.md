# Slow the breath, rebuild the trigger

> **About this document.** This is the evidence review carried out in October 2026 while Downshift was being designed. It assesses the first prototype, which sounded an alarm on a 30-bpm spike and started a two-minute exercise automatically. The app was rebuilt to follow the recommendations here. Where the text says "the app currently", it means that prototype. [evidence.md](evidence.md) lists what the app does now.

The evidence backs the app's breathing exercise far better than its alarm. The best-supported exercise is plain slow breathing at about six breaths a minute with no breath holds (4 seconds in and 6 out, or 5 and 5) for five minutes; cyclic sighing is a reasonable alternative that has not beaten any other slow technique, and two-minute sessions rest on pilot-grade data. The current alert rule is not derived from any published method: psychological stress in people who stay still raises heart rate by roughly 4 to 21 bpm for minutes at a time, whereas standing up, walking about and the WHOOP strap's own second-by-second error during posture changes produce swings of 16 to 34 bpm within seconds, so a 30-bpm jump held for 15 seconds mostly flags movement and artefact. No study validates stress detection from heart rate alone, and in the trials that tested physiologically timed prompts, stress fell just as much after randomly timed ones. The app should therefore demote the alert to a silent, question-style nudge (about +15 bpm over a seated baseline held for about five minutes, at most once an hour and three times a day, all extrapolated numbers), stop auto-starting with a chime, and stop scoring success by heart-rate drop. It should add what it lacks and what has firmer support: scheduled breaks of 5 to 10 minutes after 25 to 50 minutes of work, filled with movement, breathing or quiet rest. Students can expect to feel calmer and less tired; no trial shows that breathing prompts or any break schedule raises grades.

## Six breaths a minute for five minutes is the best-supported exercise

Two claims about breathing exercises are well supported, and both are narrower than the popular version. First, slow breathing changes cardiac physiology while it is being done: a meta-analysis of **223 studies** found vagally mediated heart rate variability rose during slow breathing (RMSSD g = 0.53), but the effect shrank to g = 0.14 immediately after a single session, and 215 of the studies were rated at high risk of bias ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)). Second, breathwork lowers self-reported stress by a small-to-medium amount: **g = -0.35 across 12 randomized trials (N = 785)**, none of them at low risk of bias, with the authors warning of "a miscalibration between hype and evidence" ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)). What is weak is the claim that one pattern is specially effective. In the largest blinded trial, 400 adults practised either 5.5 breaths a minute or a placebo pace of 12 for about ten minutes a day over four weeks; both groups improved and they did not differ on stress, anxiety, depression or wellbeing ([Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49279-8)). The defensible promise for the app is therefore modest: a few minutes of slow, guided breathing tends to make people feel calmer in the moment.

Within that limit, pace has the most consistent support. When 828 adults were randomly assigned breathing rates between 6 and 12 a minute for one ten-minute app session, slower rates predicted lower anxiety afterwards in a linear fashion ([Czub et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3496)). In 84 college students, plain breathing at six a minute raised heart rate variability more than box breathing or 4-7-8 breathing ([Marchant et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-025-09688-z)), and in 75 athletes any rate from five to seven a minute worked ([You et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-023-09605-2)). A fixed pace of **about six breaths a minute** is the tested value. Individually calibrated "resonance" pacing was the only condition that improved mood relative to sitting quietly in one 95-student trial ([Steffen et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00222)), but finding a personal resonance rate needs beat-to-beat data the app does not receive, and no trial has compared it with a fixed six-a-minute pace in an app.

The inhale-to-exhale ratio matters less than its reputation suggests. A review counted nine earlier direct tests: four favored a longer exhale, three found no difference, one favored equal breathing and one a longer inhale, most at high risk of bias. The same authors then ran the comparison twice in small trained samples with verified breathing at six a minute and found no difference in heart rate or any variability measure between 1:1 and 1:2 pacing ([Meehan & Shaffer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-024-09637-2)). A 12-week trial in 100 adults found extended-exhale and equal breathing reduced anxiety alike ([Birdee et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2023.102937)), and the 828-person experiment found no ratio effect on anxiety at all ([Czub et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3496)). The best positive evidence is a small advantage on one variability measure in 64 athletes, d = 0.37 over equal breathing ([Laborde et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/su13147775)). The app's 4-in/6-out and 5-in/5-out patterns are equally defensible, and the choice between them is a comfort setting. Strongly skewed ratios fail for a practical reason: untrained people do not achieve them, as when a 2:1 exhale cue produced an actual ratio of 1.33 ([Bae et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)).

Cyclic sighing, the app's current default, has thinner support than the plain patterns already in its menu. Its evidence is essentially one study: 108 mostly Stanford undergraduates practised one of four techniques for five minutes a day, and the 30 assigned to cyclic sighing gained more daily positive mood than the meditation group. State anxiety, the outcome closest to "calm down now", fell equally with sighing (-3.85), box breathing (-3.75) and meditation (-3.95). Resting heart rate and heart rate variability did not change in any group, the trial was registered retrospectively, WHOOP donated the straps, and the senior author later became a WHOOP adviser ([Balban et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). A 167-student conceptual replication, available only as a conference poster, found no effect on mood or anxiety ([Boucher & Dixon 2024](https://uwaterloo.ca/psychology-discovery-conference/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/boucher_pdc_2024.pdf)), and no large preregistered replication was found as of October 2026. The small 2026 studies that set sighing-type breathing beside box breathing find the two perform alike against passive controls ([Riedl et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809); [McAllister et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpnec.2026.100360)). Box breathing and 4-7-8 are in a similar position: no study located shows that breath holds add anything, and plain six-a-minute breathing beat both on heart rate variability ([Marchant et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-025-09688-z)). Sighing and box breathing can stay as options; neither earns the default slot.

Five minutes is the best-supported session length, and the two-minute default is the weakest of the app's exercise settings. A systematic review of 72 breathing interventions found that six of the nine using sessions under five minutes were ineffective (p = 0.005) while going beyond five minutes added nothing (p = 0.81); the authors caution that the short failures were also unguided, one-off or missing other core components ([Bentley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci13121612)). A direct test of 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes at six breaths a minute found the same rise in heart rate variability at every length, and it switched off when the breathing stopped ([You et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)). Below five minutes the evidence is preliminary but not empty. Two minutes of slow breathing before a 30-minute decision task kept stress from rising and raised the share of correct answers in 56 students ([De Couck et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2019.02.011)), and one minute of sighing or box breathing during real-life anxious moments reduced state anxiety more than doing nothing in a 47-student pilot ([Riedl et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809)). No study has randomized one, two, three and five minutes against each other.

Delivery matters as much as pattern. A visual pacer is sufficient, and adding live heart rate variability biofeedback changed nothing in a 112-person comparison ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13952)), so the app loses nothing in the exercise itself by lacking that signal. Prior instruction is what separates success from failure: human-guided training, which can be a short recorded video, marked the effective interventions (p < 0.001), and seven of eight device-only single sessions failed ([Bentley et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10741869/)). First exposure is the weak point. On day one of paced breathing, 37.5% of 16 novices over-breathed enough to drop end-tidal CO2 below 30 mmHg and the task was not calming ([Szulczewski 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218550)), and adolescents found app-guided slow breathing after a stressor more uncomfortable than relaxing naturally ([Schleicher et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2024.107148)). The app should teach the exercise once while the student is calm, cue gentle breaths and never "deep" ones, and make skipping easy. A nasal inhale is a sensible default because nearly every tested protocol used one and a 20-person study found nasal breathing slightly lowered diastolic pressure without changing heart rate ([Watso et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00148.2023)); no study compares exhale routes, so breathing out through the nose or the mouth is a free choice. Slow breathing is otherwise low risk, with lightheadedness the main reported complaint ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)). Fast, high-ventilation patterns are the ones with documented dizziness, tingling and tetany, and they should never be the response to a spike ([Fincham et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-64254-7)).

## A 30-bpm jump for 15 seconds flags movement, not stress

The size and shape of a real stress response do not match the alert rule. The cleanest laboratory estimate, taken with people kept still, shows heart rate rising from **70 bpm at rest to 78 while anticipating a stress test, 91 during a judged speech and 87 during mental arithmetic** ([Bigalke et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401978/)). Silent cognitive stressors do less: about +4 bpm for interference tasks and +9 bpm for timed arithmetic in samples of 154 and 212 young adults ([Cook et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12743145/)). In the one study of real exams with a wrist tracker, heart rate ran 8.9 bpm above a stress-free baseline during exams and 3.2 bpm above it during other self-reported stressors ([Pakhomov et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7094857/)). These elevations last as long as the stressor, which is minutes, and heart rate returns to baseline promptly afterwards ([Mohammadi et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6821413/)). A threshold of +30 bpm therefore sits above the average response to the strongest standard laboratory stressor and at more than three times the response to an exam.

Ordinary movement clears that bar easily, on exactly the 15-second timescale the rule uses. Standing up from a chair takes heart rate from 62 to 78 bpm within about 8 seconds and the transient is over within 30 ([Heidelbach et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13018822/)). Quiet standing holds it about 13 bpm above sitting ([Júdice et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26467968/)), light activity added 18.6 bpm in the same students whose exams added 8.9 ([Pakhomov et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7094857/)), and a long stair climb reaches about 159 bpm ([Teh & Aziz 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11932581/)). The sensor adds its own noise at these moments. WHOOP 4.0 is accurate at rest, with about 3% error and better still on the upper arm ([Moghaddam et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12788198/)). Against a 12-lead ECG in a sit, stand and walk protocol, however, its **per-second readings had limits of agreement of about ±34 bpm, and still about ±24 bpm after 10-second averaging**, and it was one of two devices that "performed poorly in all transitions" ([Van Oost et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12568089/)). A 30-bpm excursion lasting 15 seconds is inside the strap's error band during a posture change. These studies used data exported from WHOOP's own app; no validation of the Bluetooth broadcast the tray app reads was found.

The deeper problem is that heart rate alone cannot identify stress at any threshold. No study located validates a stress detector that uses mean heart rate without motion data or beat-to-beat intervals in daily life. Published methods remove movement first, and one chest-strap study saw field accuracy fall from an F1 of 0.62 to 0.51 when active periods were left in ([Mishra et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7442214/)). Full sensor suites still struggle outside the lab: among 1,002 office workers wearing chest and wrist sensors, the average F1 was 0.43 against 0.36 for always guessing the majority class ([Smets et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6550211/)). The classic "additional heart rate" algorithm, which had accelerometers, produced alerts that matched reported emotion no better than control prompts ([Brouwer et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6054929/)). Commercial stress features, WHOOP's own included, by the makers' descriptions combine heart rate variability with motion and a multi-day personal baseline, 14 days in WHOOP's case ([WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/thelocker/introducing-stress-monitor-a-new-way-to-monitor-manage-stress/)). The plain high-heart-rate alerts on Apple and Garmin watches require ten minutes of device-confirmed inactivity and are framed as heart-health notices ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-gw/guide/watch/heart-rate-apda88aefe4c/6.0); [Garmin](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-C144B465-A0C8-4FE9-AFE6-41A3FE3F1D9A/EN-US/GUID-F00E423A-165E-4557-91EE-DC2EDF737E79.html)). The 130-bpm rule fits neither model. Speech-phase heart rate without a posture change averaged 91 ± 18 bpm ([Bigalke et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401978/)), and the 95th percentile of real-world readings for ages 18 to 45 is at most 110 bpm ([Avram et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6592896/)). In a student who is truly sitting still, 130 bpm is unusual enough to be a wellbeing question; in one who is not, it is just exertion.

Even a perfect detector would have an unproven payoff. In two preregistered micro-randomized trials with 60 and 50 mostly student participants, a one-minute exercise reduced perceived stress equally whether the prompt was triggered by a movement-adjusted drop in heart rate variability or sent at random. The trigger itself needed 13 flagged minutes inside roughly 28 and was followed by a 60-minute silent period ([Schwerdtfeger et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12371293/)). In a four-week workplace study of 86 people, system-triggered prompts led to more completed exercises but no greater stress reduction than self-scheduled ones, and users wanted partial control over timing ([Howe et al. 2022](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/design-of-digital-workplace-stress-reduction-intervention-systems-effects-of-intervention-type-and-timing)). The realistic value of a heart-rate cue is as a reminder that raises engagement. Against that sit the costs of a false alarm during study: interruptions of under three seconds doubled procedural errors ([Altmann et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23294345/)), a notification that is never answered still impairs an attention task ([Stothart et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/)), and people interrupted every two minutes during a laboratory email task reported markedly more stress, 9.5 against 6.9 on a 20-point scale ([Mark et al. 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)). A chime followed by an auto-started exercise, repeatable every ten minutes, is far more aggressive than any design that has been studied.

A raised heart rate is also not a sign that thinking is about to suffer. Threat-type stress produces a small impairment in working memory (g = -0.20, concentrated in high-load tasks) but improves response inhibition ([Shields et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.06.038)), arousal from exercise slightly helps cognition ([Chang et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2012.02.068)), and a critical review concluded the inverted-U "Yerkes-Dodson law" has no basis in empirical fact as a law of human stress and performance ([Corbett 2015](https://doi.org/10.1108/JMP-03-2013-0085)). The harmful ingredient is evaluative threat, which a heart rate number cannot see. The alert should ask the student how they feel; it should not tell them.

The end-of-session score has the same flaw as the trigger. Pooled across non-clinical studies, mean heart rate after slow breathing changes by about a tenth of a standard deviation, measured within groups with no control ([Shao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2)), and a month of daily cyclic sighing left resting heart rate unchanged ([Balban et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). In students prompted to breathe for five minutes in daily life, perceived stress fell while heart rate variability did not differ from no-exercise control times ([Bamert et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2026.2653702)). Because a spike also decays on its own, a heart-rate drop after the exercise credits the breathing for what movement recovery did, and a flat reading reports failure when the student feels better. The trials measured how people felt, and the app should do the same.

The supported moments for a breathing exercise are therefore three, and none depends on a spike. The first is the few minutes before a demanding block or test, where the cognitive findings sit. The second is whenever the student says they feel tense. The third is inside a scheduled break. A sustained seated elevation is a reason to ask, and nothing more.

## Scheduled breaks rest on firmer ground than heart-rate alerts

Breaks reliably improve how people feel and only weakly improve what they produce. A meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335) found breaks of ten minutes or less raised vigour (d = 0.36) and cut fatigue (d = 0.35), while the pooled performance effect was d = 0.16 and not significant, with no benefit detected for cognitively demanding tasks and larger gains from longer breaks inside the ten-minute range ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)). Controlled experiments agree that a real rest restores attention better than continuing or filling the gap with another demanding task ([Helton & Russell 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.10.001); [Helton & Russell 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816683509)), and that merely switching goals does not protect performance ([Ross et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-014-3865-5)), so changing subject does not count as a break. Timing matters at the long end: a 12-minute break after about 90 minutes of continuous demanding work did not restore performance ([Matuz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02429)), and in real four-hour classes fatigue rose within 30 minutes without a break (d = 0.44) ([Blasche et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.2830)). Motivated students nevertheless held their test performance through 3.5 to 5.5 hours while fatigue climbed ([Ackerman & Kanfer 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015719)). For a student, the measurable cost of skipping breaks is tiredness and disengagement, not an immediate drop in accuracy.

No controlled study establishes an optimal work interval. The only schedules tested in randomized student experiments are 24 or 25 minutes of work with 5 or 6 minutes of rest, and 12 minutes with 3, each compared with breaks the student chose freely. In 87 students over one day, fixed breaks gave less fatigue and better concentration with the same work completed in less time ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593)). In 94 students over two hours, 25/5 made no overall difference ([Smits et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15070861)). In 176 students, 12/3 produced no significant difference in reading comprehension (d = 0.24, p = .056) and worse mood, and students said 12-minute blocks broke their concentration ([Göksu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071158)). The famous alternatives are folklore. The 52/17 rule comes from a time-tracking vendor's blog with no stated sample, and the same company later reported 112/26 and 80/17 ([DeskTime](https://desktime.com/blog/17-52-ratio-most-productive-people)). The 90-minute "ultradian" block is an extension of a sleep-cycle concept to waking work ([Kleitman 1982](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/5.4.311)). The claim that attention collapses 10 to 15 minutes into a lecture is not supported by primary data ([Bradbury 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016)). For the longer pause, Danish national test data show scores falling 0.9% of a standard deviation for each hour later in the day and recovering 1.7% after a **20-to-30-minute break** ([Sievertsen et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)).

What fills the break matters less than taking it, but a pattern holds. In 66 students, exercise breaks and guided relaxation breaks of six to seven minutes both beat an unstructured break: exercise added vigour (d = 0.57) and relaxation lowered fatigue 20 minutes later ([Blasche et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.2830)). A single bout of exercise gives a small cognitive lift, g = 0.13 across 113 studies of young adults ([Garrett et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-024-00124-2)). A few minutes of quiet rest straight after new material yields a small memory benefit in young adults, g = 0.20, or 0.15 once outliers are removed ([Parra et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02778-3)). The case against phones rests on one experiment in 414 undergraduates, in which a phone-based break left students solving fewer problems afterwards than other breaks did (6.4 against 8.1) ([Kang & Kurtzberg 2019](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.8.2019.21)). It agrees with the stronger finding that demanding break activities restore less. Slow breathing lowers felt arousal ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13952)), so it suits a tense student and is the wrong tool for a sleepy one, who is better served by movement.

Breathing has its most direct cognitive evidence at one point in this rhythm, which is the last minutes of a break before hard work resumes. Three five-minute bouts before testing improved Stroop accuracy and working-memory scores in 78 young adults, with no gain in speed and no mediation by heart rate variability ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1027/0269-8803/a000279)). One session improved working memory in 139 undergraduates, with the mood benefit gone once the task was done ([Pilcher et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2505897)). The effect was confined to more-stressed students in a 216-person trial ([Byrne et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2025.105222)) and absent in a 48-person crossover ([Liang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1155134)). No meta-analysis of these cognitive outcomes exists, only a registered protocol ([Ramquar et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-025-03004-w)). The pattern is plausible and unconfirmed.

A break prompt has to be firm without being rigid. Notifications deferred to a natural breakpoint, by 89 seconds on average, produced less frustration than immediate ones in one of two task types ([Iqbal & Bailey 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357070)), and mid-task interruptions raised annoyance by 56% over between-task ones ([Adamczyk & Bailey 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985727)). When declining is free, people decline: of 1,599 desktop break prompts sent to 25 office workers, 40% were accepted, 34% declined and 26% ignored, and accepted breaks often overran ([Luo et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173701)). Left alone, students drift into longer sessions and longer breaks with more fatigue ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593)). Jotting down where one stopped and what comes next reduces the mental residue of an interrupted task ([Leroy & Glomb 2018](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184)). The often-quoted "23 minutes to refocus" describes how long office workers took to return to an interrupted project with other tasks in between ([Gallup 2006](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx)); it says nothing about a planned five-minute rest.

The strap should not decide when breaks happen. Over 90 minutes of demanding work, heart rate drifts down and vagal variability drifts up, and many of the same indices also shift while simply watching documentaries ([Matuz et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0238670)). In the one randomized test, a heart rate variability index identified mentally fatigued adults with an AUC of 0.62, worse than a single self-rating of fatigue at 0.69 ([Yoshikawa et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000240)). The clock and a one-tap rating are the better instruments.

## What the app should do, setting by setting

### How firm each claim is

| Strength | Claims |
|---|---|
| Well supported | Slow breathing near 6 breaths/min raises heart rate variability while it is performed. Breathing exercises give small-to-medium reductions in self-reported stress against mostly passive controls. Short breaks reduce fatigue and raise vigour. A real rest restores more than continuing or a demanding filler. Movement raises heart rate as much as or more than stress does, and wrist sensors are least accurate during posture changes. Interruptions cost errors and stress. |
| Weak or mixed | Any one technique (cyclic sighing, box, "coherent" 5.5/min) beating another. A longer exhale beating an equal one. Sessions shorter than 5 minutes. A single breathing session improving attention or memory. Fixed break schedules beating self-chosen ones. Micro-breaks improving performance on demanding work. Phone breaks being harmful. Physiologically timed prompts beating random or scheduled ones. Pre-test reappraisal of nerves. |
| Unproven or folklore | Detecting stress from heart rate alone, including +30 bpm for 15 s and 130 bpm. A drop in heart rate as proof the exercise worked. Breath holds or 4-7-8 adding benefit. Heart rate or HRV deciding when to break. 52/17, 90-minute ultradian blocks, 50/10 as an optimum, the 10-to-15-minute attention span, "23 minutes to refocus". Breathing breaks or any break schedule raising grades. |

Two consequences of these settings should be stated to the student inside the app. The rebuilt heart-rate nudge will stay silent through most real study stress, because exam-type strain averages under +10 bpm and sits below any threshold that movement does not swamp. And when it does fire it still cannot tell tension from a walk to the kitchen, a phone call or a laugh, which is why it asks a question and offers "I was moving" as an answer. That answer is also the app's calibration data: logged beside the tension ratings collected at ordinary breaks, it shows within a few weeks whether heart-rate prompts land on tense moments more often than clock-timed ones do.

If only three changes are made, the order is clear. Adding the break timer brings in the component with the strongest evidence. Lengthening the sustain time to minutes, the wait to an hour and replacing the chime and auto-start with a silent question removes most false alarms and most of their cost. Switching the default to five minutes of 4-in/6-out breathing and replacing the heart-rate score with a self-rating aligns the exercise with what the trials tested and measured.

## What the student can realistically expect for grades and exams

In plain terms, this app can help a student feel better while studying. It has not been shown to make anyone's marks go up.

The realistic gains are about comfort. A few minutes of slow breathing usually leaves people feeling calmer for a short while, and the feeling fades once hard work resumes ([Pilcher et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2505897)). Regular short breaks leave people less tired and more energetic by a modest amount, without reliably improving performance on demanding mental work ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)). Some small experiments find slightly fewer mistakes on attention and memory tests taken right after slow breathing, but one found nothing, the tests were laboratory puzzles and not schoolwork, and nobody has yet pooled the results ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1027/0269-8803/a000279); [Liang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1155134)).

For grades themselves the evidence is close to empty. No randomized trial was found that tests breathing prompts or break timers against real exam marks. The one study linking a breathing-and-relaxation program to better university marks was not described as randomized ([Aritzeta et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-017-9367-z)). Deep breaths just before a timed maths test improved scores in one study of 122 primary-school pupils that has not been directly repeated ([Khng 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2016.1233095)), and yogic breathing before a final exam made no difference to anxiety in 119 nursing students ([Ortega-Donaire et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11040574)). Other popular pre-exam fixes have shrunk on closer inspection. Writing about worries shows a negligible effect across 21 studies ([O'Meara & Lovett 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857)). Telling yourself that nerves help has a small average benefit, d of 0.23 falling to 0.14 after correction for publication bias ([Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)), and a 2026 replication across seven institutions found no effect ([Thormodsæter et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.25-04-0055)).

Nervous students do tend to score lower, but mostly not because they freeze on the day. Among 309 medical students, exam anxiety stopped predicting the final score once practice-exam results were taken into account, and anxious students had gained less knowledge during the preparation weeks ([Theobald et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221119391)). The habits with strong evidence for marks are unglamorous: testing yourself and spreading study of the same material across days ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)), and sleeping long and regularly for weeks, since the single night before a test showed no relation to the score ([Okano et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-019-0055-z)). A fair expectation is that the app makes long study sessions more comfortable and easier to sustain, helps most for students who get tense, and leaves the grade to depend on what is done between the breaks.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the app's design. The part built as the centrepiece, a heart-rate alarm, is the part with no validation, and the part it lacks, a break rhythm, is the part with meta-analytic support. The strap still earns its place, though in a different role. It is a weak detector, a serviceable reminder, and a good logging instrument, and the third role is the most interesting. No trial of heart-rate-triggered breathing prompts in studying students exists, so every student who uses the app is running that experiment on themselves. If the app records a one-tap rating at heart-rate prompts, at scheduled breaks and at self-started sessions, it produces the comparison the literature is missing, and it gives a principled reason to switch the heart-rate nudge off for any student in whom it proves no better than the clock.

Three questions would change these recommendations if answered. A head-to-head trial of one, two and five minutes would settle whether the quick option deserves to be the default. A preregistered replication of cyclic sighing would settle whether its mood advantage is real. And the notes behind this report did not verify whether the strap's broadcast can carry beat-to-beat intervals; every published stress-detection method depends on them, so their availability would reopen the detection question that heart rate alone has closed.
