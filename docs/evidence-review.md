# Historical prototype review: slow the breath, rebuild the trigger

> **About this document.** This is the evidence review carried out in October 2026 while Downshift was being designed. It assesses the first prototype, which sounded an alarm on a 30-bpm spike and started a two-minute exercise automatically. The app was then rebuilt with these findings as input, but it does not follow every recommendation here (for example, it has no warning after 90 minutes without a break) and some of its settings differ. Where the text says "the app currently", it means that prototype. [evidence.md](evidence.md) lists what the app does now.

The evidence backs the app's breathing exercise far better than its alarm. The best-supported exercise is plain slow breathing at about six breaths a minute with no breath holds (4 seconds in and 6 out, or 5 and 5) for five minutes. Cyclic sighing is a reasonable alternative that has not beaten any other slow technique, and two-minute sessions rest on pilot-grade data. The current alert rule has no published method behind it that we could find. Psychological stress in people who stay still raises heart rate by roughly 4 to 21 bpm for minutes at a time, whereas standing up raises heart rate by about 16 bpm within seconds, and the WHOOP strap's own per-second error had 95% limits of about ±34 bpm in one validation. A 30-bpm jump held for 15 seconds is therefore more likely to reflect movement or sensor error than stress. No study we know of validates stress detection from heart rate alone, and in the trials that tested physiologically timed prompts, stress fell just as much after randomly timed ones. The app should demote the alert to a silent, question-style nudge (about +15 bpm over a seated baseline held for about five minutes, at most once an hour and three times a day, all extrapolated numbers), stop auto-starting with a chime, and stop scoring success by heart-rate drop. It should add what it lacks and what has firmer support: scheduled breaks of 5 to 10 minutes after 25 to 50 minutes of work, filled with movement, breathing or quiet rest. Students can expect to feel calmer and less tired. Whether breathing prompts or any break schedule raise grades is untested in any trial we could find.

## Six breaths a minute for five minutes is the best-supported exercise

Two claims about breathing exercises are well supported, and both are narrower than the popular version. First, slow breathing changes cardiac physiology while it is being done: a meta-analysis of **223 studies** found vagally mediated heart rate variability rose during slow breathing, immediately after a single session and after multi-session programs (abstract only; the pooled effect sizes and risk-of-bias ratings could not be re-checked) ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)). Second, breathwork lowers self-reported stress by a small-to-medium amount: **g = -0.35 across 12 randomized trials (N = 785)**, most at moderate risk of bias, with the authors warning of "a miscalibration between hype and evidence" ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)). What is weak is the claim that one pattern is specially effective. In the largest blinded trial, 400 adults practiced either 5.5 breaths a minute or a placebo pace of 12 for about ten minutes a day over four weeks; both groups improved and they did not differ on stress, anxiety, depression or well-being ([Fincham, Strauss & Cavanagh 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-49279-8)). The defensible promise for the app is therefore modest: a few minutes of slow, guided breathing tends to make people feel calmer in the moment.

Within that limit, pace has the most consistent support. When 828 adults were randomly assigned breathing rates between 6 and 12 a minute for one ten-minute app session, slower rates predicted lower anxiety afterwards in a linear fashion ([Czub et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3496)). In 84 college students, plain breathing at six a minute raised heart rate variability more than box breathing or 4-7-8 breathing ([Marchant et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-025-09688-z)), and in 75 athletes any rate from five to seven a minute raised vagally mediated heart rate variability ([You et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-023-09605-2)). A fixed pace of **about six breaths a minute** is the tested value. Individually calibrated "resonance" pacing was the only condition that improved mood relative to sitting quietly in one 95-student trial ([Steffen et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00222)), but finding a personal resonance rate typically needs beat-to-beat data, which the app does not use, and we haven't seen a trial comparing it with a fixed six-a-minute pace in an app.

The inhale-to-exhale ratio matters less than its reputation suggests. A review counted nine earlier direct tests: four favored a longer exhale, three found no difference, one favored equal breathing and one a longer inhale, several at high risk of bias. The same authors then ran the comparison twice in small samples (26 and 16 people) at six breaths a minute and found no difference in any heart rate variability measure between 1:1 and 1:2 pacing ([Meehan & Shaffer 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-024-09637-2)). In a 12-week trial in 100 adults, both extended-exhale and equal breathing reduced anxiety, with no significant difference between them (d = 0.2 favoring the longer exhale) ([Birdee et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2023.102937)), and the 828-person experiment found no significant relationship between ratio and anxiety ([Czub et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3496)). The best positive evidence is a higher RMSSD when 64 athletes exhaled longer than they inhaled, at six breaths a minute (abstract only; effect size not re-checked) ([Laborde et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/su13147775)). The app's 4-in/6-out and 5-in/5-out patterns are equally defensible, and the choice between them is a comfort setting. Strongly skewed ratios may fail for a practical reason: untrained people may not achieve them, as in one study of 28 people where a 2:1 cue produced an actual ratio of 1.33 ([Bae et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13905)).

Cyclic sighing, the app's current default, has thinner support than the plain patterns already in its menu. Its evidence is essentially one study: 108 mostly Stanford undergraduates practiced one of four techniques for five minutes a day, and the 30 assigned to cyclic sighing gained more daily positive mood than the meditation group. State anxiety, the outcome closest to "calm down now", fell equally with sighing (-3.85), box breathing (-3.75) and meditation (-3.95). Resting heart rate and heart rate variability did not change in any group, the trial was not preregistered, WHOOP donated the straps, and the senior author became a WHOOP adviser in June 2022 ([Balban et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). A 167-participant study with cyclic sighing, positive-affirmation and no-treatment groups (5 minutes a day for 20 days), available only as a conference poster that has not been peer reviewed, found that cyclic sighing lowered resting breathing rate and raised resting heart rate variability relative to no treatment, and that this did not carry over to mood or anxiety measures ([Boucher & Dixon 2024](https://uwaterloo.ca/psychology-discovery-conference/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/boucher_pdc_2024.pdf); poster read in full), and as of October 2026 we know of no large preregistered replication. In a 47-student pilot across three arms, cyclic sighing and box breathing were both associated with greater reductions in state anxiety, but also with longer reaction times afterwards ([Riedl et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809)), and in a 66-person trial box breathing and prolonged exhalation performed alike ([McAllister et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpnec.2026.100360)). Box breathing and 4-7-8 are in a similar position: nothing we located shows that breath holds add anything, and plain six-a-minute breathing beat both on heart rate variability ([Marchant et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-025-09688-z)). Sighing and box breathing can stay as options; neither earns the default slot.

Five minutes is the best-supported session length, and the two-minute default is the weakest of the app's exercise settings. A systematic review of 72 breathing interventions found that six of the nine using sessions under five minutes were ineffective (p = 0.005) while going beyond five minutes was not associated with further benefit (p = 0.81); the authors caution that the short failures were also unguided, one-off or missing other core components ([Bentley et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci13121612)). A direct test of 5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes at six breaths a minute in 59 adults found the same rise in heart rate variability at every length ([You et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph182312478)). Below five minutes the evidence is preliminary but not empty. Two minutes of slow breathing with a longer exhale before a 30-minute decision task kept stress from rising and raised the share of correct answers in 56 students ([De Couck et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2019.02.011)), and one minute of sighing or box breathing during real-life anxious moments was associated with greater reductions in state anxiety than doing nothing in a 47-student pilot, alongside slower reaction times ([Riedl et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2026.2659809)). We couldn't find a study that randomized one, two, three and five minutes against each other.

Delivery matters as well as pattern. A visual pacer is sufficient, and adding live heart rate variability biofeedback changed only emotional valence in a single session in a 112-person comparison ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13952)), so the app loses little in the exercise itself by lacking that signal. Prior instruction was one of several factors associated with success in a vote-counting review: human-guided training, which can be a short recorded video, marked the effective interventions (p < 0.001), and seven of eight single-session interventions using simple device-guided practices were ineffective ([Bentley et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10741869/)). First exposure is the weak point. On day one of paced breathing, 37.5% of 16 novices over-breathed enough to drop end-tidal CO2 below 30 mmHg and the task was not calming ([Szulczewski 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0218550)), and in 73 adolescents app-guided slow breathing after a stressor may have felt more uncomfortable than relaxing naturally ([Schleicher et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2024.107148)). The app should teach the exercise once while the student is calm, cue easy breaths and never "deep" ones, and make skipping easy. A nasal inhale is a sensible default: a 20-person study found nasal breathing slightly lowered diastolic pressure without changing heart rate ([Watso et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00148.2023)). Exhale routes appear untested, so breathing out through the nose or the mouth is a free choice. Slow breathing is otherwise low risk, with few adverse effects expected ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711)). Fast, high-ventilation breathwork with breath holds showed no benefit over a placebo comparator in 200 young adults, and a few participants in both arms reported light-headedness or dizziness, with tingling and tetany-like symptoms also reported in the fast-breathing arm ([Fincham et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-64254-7)); nothing supports using it as the response to a spike.

## A 30-bpm jump for 15 seconds is more likely movement than stress

The size and shape of a real stress response do not match the alert rule. The cleanest laboratory estimate, taken with people lying semi-reclined, shows heart rate rising from **70 bpm at rest to 78 while anticipating a stress test, 91 during a judged speech and 87 during mental arithmetic** ([Bigalke et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401978/)). Silent cognitive stressors do less: about +4 bpm for interference tasks and +9 bpm for a timed serial-addition task in samples of 154 and 212 young adults ([Cook et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12743145/)). In the one study of real exams with a wrist tracker that we found, heart rate ran 8.9 bpm above a stress-free baseline around the exams they reported (3.9 bpm in the first 20 minutes of scheduled exams) and 3.2 bpm above it during other self-reported stressors ([Pakhomov et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7094857/)). These elevations last as long as the stressor, which is minutes; after a laboratory stress test some heart rate variability measures had still not returned to baseline 20 minutes later ([Mohammadi et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6821413/)), so stress physiology outlasts the moment rather than flickering for seconds. A threshold of +30 bpm therefore sits above the average response to the strongest standard laboratory stressor and at more than three times the response to an exam.

Ordinary movement gets about halfway to that bar within seconds, on the 15-second timescale the rule uses, and sustained effort goes far past it. Standing up from a chair takes heart rate from 62 to 78 bpm within about 8 seconds, and the transient has mostly but not fully settled by 30 seconds (about 71 bpm) ([Heidelbach et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13018822/)). Quiet standing holds it about 13 bpm above sitting ([Júdice et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26467968/)), physical activity added 18.6 bpm in the same students whose exams added 8.9 ([Pakhomov et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7094857/)), and a long stair climb reaches about 159 bpm ([Teh & Aziz 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11932581/)). The sensor adds its own noise at these moments. WHOOP 4.0 is accurate at rest, with about 3% error and better still on the upper arm ([Moghaddam et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12788198/)). In a sit, stand and walk protocol checked against a 12-lead ECG, however, its **per-second readings had limits of agreement of about ±34 bpm, and still about ±24 bpm after 10-second averaging** (about ±25 bpm per second against a chest-strap reference, and 57% of its per-second pairs with the ECG were missing), and it was one of two devices that "performed poorly in all transitions" ([Van Oost et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12568089/)). A 30-bpm excursion lasting 15 seconds is comparable to the strap's overall per-second error band (about ±34 bpm over the protocol). Van Oost recorded WHOOP through its own app; [Moghaddam et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/s26010176) captured WHOOP 4.0 heart rate over Bluetooth in a third-party app during cycling, burpees and a treadmill test. We haven't seen a validation of the broadcast during lifting, or of WHOOP 5.0 or MG.

The deeper problem is that heart rate alone has not been shown to identify stress at any threshold. Among the studies we located, none validates a stress detector that uses mean heart rate without motion data or beat-to-beat intervals in daily life. Published methods remove movement first, and one chest-strap study saw field accuracy fall from an F1 of 0.62 to 0.51 when active periods were left in ([Mishra et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7442214/)). Full sensor suites still struggle outside the lab: in a cohort of 1,002 office workers wearing chest and wrist sensors, analyzed for the 568 with good-quality, low-movement data, the average F1 was 0.43 against 0.36 for always guessing the majority class ([Smets et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6550211/)). In the studies of the classic "additional heart rate" algorithm, which used accelerometers, reported emotion did not differ between prompts the algorithm triggered and prompts it did not (as reviewed by [Brouwer et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6054929/)). WHOOP's stress feature, by WHOOP's description, compares heart rate and heart rate variability with a 14-day personal baseline and takes motion into account ([WHOOP](https://www.whoop.com/thelocker/introducing-stress-monitor-a-new-way-to-monitor-manage-stress/)). The plain high-heart-rate alerts on Apple and Garmin watches are user-set threshold alerts that fire after ten minutes of inactivity ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-gw/guide/watch/heart-rate-apda88aefe4c/6.0); [Garmin](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-C144B465-A0C8-4FE9-AFE6-41A3FE3F1D9A/EN-US/GUID-F00E423A-165E-4557-91EE-DC2EDF737E79.html)). The 130-bpm rule fits neither model. Speech-phase heart rate without a posture change averaged 91 ± 18 bpm ([Bigalke et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401978/)), and the 95th percentile of real-world readings for ages 18 to 45 is at most 110 bpm ([Avram et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6592896/)). In a student who is truly sitting still, 130 bpm is unusual enough to be a well-being question; in one who is not, it is just exertion.

Even a perfect detector would have an unproven payoff. In two preregistered micro-randomized trials with 60 and 50 mostly student participants, perceived stress fell after a one-minute exercise equally whether the prompt was triggered by a movement-adjusted drop in heart rate variability or sent at random. The trigger itself needed 13 flagged minutes inside roughly 28 and was followed by a 60-minute silent period; heart rate variability rose more over the 10 minutes afterwards in the triggered condition than in the random one, in both trials ([Schwerdtfeger et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12371293/)). In a four-week workplace study of 86 people, system-triggered prompts led to more completed exercises but no greater stress reduction than self-scheduled ones, and users wanted partial control over timing ([Howe et al. 2022](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/design-of-digital-workplace-stress-reduction-intervention-systems-effects-of-intervention-type-and-timing)). What a heart-rate cue can realistically offer is a reminder that raises engagement. Against that sit the costs of a false alarm during study: interruptions averaging 2.8 seconds doubled sequence (place-keeping) errors ([Altmann et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23294345/)), a notification that is never answered still impairs an attention task ([Stothart et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/)), and people interrupted every two minutes during a laboratory email task reported markedly more stress, 9.5 against 6.9 on a 20-point scale ([Mark et al. 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)). A chime followed by an auto-started exercise, repeatable every ten minutes, is more intrusive than the designs we reviewed (for example, Schwerdtfeger's 60-minute silent period).

A raised heart rate is also not a sign that thinking is about to suffer. Acute laboratory stress produces a small impairment in working memory (g = -0.20, larger in high-load tasks) and improves response inhibition (while impairing cognitive inhibition) ([Shields et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.06.038)), arousal from exercise slightly helps cognition ([Chang et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2012.02.068)), and a critical review concluded the inverted-U "Yerkes-Dodson law" has no basis in empirical fact as a law of human stress and performance ([Corbett 2015](https://doi.org/10.1108/JMP-03-2013-0085)). Evaluative threat is a plausible harmful ingredient (the test-anxiety studies cited here are correlational), and a heart rate number cannot see it. The alert should ask the student how they feel; it should not tell them.

The end-of-session score has the same flaw as the trigger. Pooled across non-clinical studies, slow breathing lowered heart rate from before to after the intervention by only about a tenth of a standard deviation (8 studies), with no significant change during the session ([Shao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-023-02294-2)), and a month of daily cyclic sighing left resting heart rate unchanged ([Balban et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). In students prompted to breathe for five minutes in daily life, perceived stress fell while heart rate variability did not differ from no-exercise control times ([Bamert et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2026.2653702)). Because a spike also decays on its own, a heart-rate drop after the exercise credits the breathing for what movement recovery did, and a flat reading reports failure when the student feels better. The trials measured how people felt, and the app should do the same.

That leaves three moments to offer a breathing exercise, and none depends on a spike. The first is the few minutes before a demanding block or test, where small trials, not yet confirmed, report cognitive benefits. The second is whenever the student says they feel tense. The third is inside a scheduled break. A sustained seated elevation is a reason to ask, and nothing more.

## Scheduled breaks rest on firmer ground than heart-rate alerts

Breaks reliably improve how people feel and only weakly improve what they produce. A meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335) found breaks of ten minutes or less raised vigor (d = 0.36) and cut fatigue (d = 0.35), while the pooled performance effect was d = 0.16 and not significant, with no benefit detected for cognitively demanding tasks and larger performance effects from longer breaks inside the ten-minute range, though break length did not change the effect on vigor or fatigue ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)). In vigilance experiments from one lab, a real rest restored attention better than continuing or filling the gap with another demanding task ([Helton & Russell 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.10.001); [Helton & Russell 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816683509)), and another experiment from that lab found that merely switching goals did not protect performance ([Ross et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-014-3865-5)), though another found a brief goal switch prevented the decrement ([Ariga & Lleras 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.12.007)), so changing subject may not count as a break. A long run without a break may be a problem, though no study we found compared that break with an earlier one: a 12-minute break after about 90 minutes of continuous demanding work did not restore performance ([Matuz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02429)), and in real four-hour classes fatigue rose within 30 minutes without a break (d = 0.44) ([Blasche et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.2830)). First-year university students nevertheless did not perform worse on 3.5-, 4.5- and 5.5-hour test batteries (mean scores were higher on the longest) while fatigue climbed ([Ackerman & Kanfer 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015719)). For a student, the measurable cost of skipping breaks in these studies is tiredness and lower vigor, not an immediate drop in accuracy.

No controlled study we could find establishes an optimal work interval. The schedules we found tested in student experiments are 24 or 25 minutes of work with 5 or 6 minutes of rest, and 12 minutes with 3, each compared with breaks the student chose freely. In 87 students over one day, fixed breaks (24/6 or 12/3) gave less fatigue and better concentration with the same work completed in less time ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593)). In 94 students over two hours, 25/5 made no overall difference in fatigue, motivation, productivity or task completion, although fatigue rose and motivation fell faster ([Smits et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15070861)). In 176 students, 12/3 produced no significant difference in reading comprehension (d = 0.24, p = .056) and worse mood, and several students said the frequent 3-minute breaks broke their concentration ([Göksu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16071158)). The famous alternatives are folklore. The 52/17 rule comes from a time-tracking vendor's blog post on the top 10% of its users by share of time in productive apps, over one workday, and the same company later reported 112/26 ([DeskTime](https://desktime.com/blog/52-17)). The 90-minute "ultradian" block borrows from Kleitman's proposed basic rest-activity cycle, which he described in the alternation of non-REM and REM sleep and as "a similar periodicity in wakefulness"; the abstract gives no work-block length ([Kleitman 1982](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/5.4.311), abstract only). The claim that attention collapses 10 to 15 minutes into a lecture is not supported by primary data ([Bradbury 2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016)). For the longer pause, Danish national test data show scores falling 0.9% of a standard deviation for each hour later in the day and recovering 1.7% after a **20-to-30-minute break** ([Sievertsen et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1516947113)).

What fills the break shows a pattern. In 66 students, exercise breaks and guided relaxation breaks of six to seven minutes both improved on an unstructured break, on different outcomes: exercise added vigor (d = 0.57) and relaxation lowered fatigue 20 minutes later ([Blasche et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.2830)). A single bout of exercise gives a small cognitive lift, g = 0.13 across 113 studies of young adults ([Garrett et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-024-00124-2)). A brief period of quiet rest straight after new material yields a small memory benefit in healthy young and middle-aged adults, g = 0.20, or 0.15 once outliers are removed, though many individual studies in this group found no effect ([Parra et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02778-3)). The case against phones rests on one experiment in 414 undergraduates, in which a phone-based break left students solving fewer problems afterwards than other breaks did (6.4 against 8.1, though still above the 5.3 of students who took no break) ([Kang & Kurtzberg 2019](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.8.2019.21)). It agrees with the stronger finding that demanding break activities restore less. Slow breathing is reported to lower felt arousal in pre-post comparisons ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.13952)), and other reviews report that it can raise alertness; matching it to a tense student and movement to a sleepy one is a design inference.

Breathing has its most direct cognitive evidence at one point in this rhythm, which is the last minutes of a break before hard work resumes. Three five-minute bouts before testing improved Stroop accuracy and working-memory scores in 78 young adults, with no gain in speed and no mediation by heart rate variability ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1027/0269-8803/a000279)). One session improved working memory in 139 undergraduates, with the mood benefit gone once the task was done ([Pilcher et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2505897)). In a 216-person trial the effect on decision-making depended on perceived stress ([Byrne et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2025.105222)), and in a 48-person crossover there was no average effect, apart from higher accuracy in men at the fifth minute ([Liang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1155134)). Our searches turned up no meta-analysis of these cognitive outcomes, only a published protocol ([Ramquar et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-025-03004-w)). The pattern is plausible and unconfirmed.

A break prompt has to be firm without being rigid. Notifications deferred to a breakpoint (mean delay 88.6 s, SD 139.3 s) were answered somewhat faster than immediate ones (3.07 s against 4.08 s, relevant notifications only, reported as p < .056), and in diagram editing, but not in programming, medium breakpoints produced less frustration than immediate delivery (2.6 against 4.5 on a 7-point scale) ([Iqbal & Bailey 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357070)). In 16 people, interruptions at the predicted best moments, between actions, were rated less annoying than those at the predicted worst moments, in the middle of an action (the paper puts the difference at 56%, described as a reduction in its abstract and a rise in its results) ([Adamczyk & Bailey 2004](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985727)). When declining is free, people often decline: in a three-week field study of 25 information workers, 1,599 break prompts were answered "yes" 40% of the time (642), "no" 34% (542) and ignored 26% (415) ([Luo et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3173701)). Left alone, students drift into longer sessions and longer breaks with more fatigue ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593)). When people expected to resume an interrupted task under time pressure, briefly planning their return (a "ready-to-resume" plan) reduced attention residue without hurting performance on the interrupting task, across four studies ([Leroy & Glomb 2018](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184), abstract only). The often-quoted "23 minutes to refocus" is the average time 36 managers, analysts, developers, engineers and project leaders took to resume interrupted work that was resumed the same day (82% of it), with about two other tasks in between ([Gallup 2006](https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/23146/too-many-interruptions-work.aspx)); it says nothing about a planned five-minute rest.

The strap should not decide when breaks happen. Over 90 minutes of demanding work, heart rate drifts down and vagal variability drifts up, and some of the same variability indices also rose, more weakly, in people simply watching documentaries ([Matuz et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0238670)). In the one randomized test we found, a heart rate variability index identified adults who had done a calculation task as fatigued with an AUC of 0.62, against 0.69 for a single self-rating of fatigue, which the authors call similar ([Yoshikawa et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000240)). The clock and a one-tap rating are simpler instruments.

## What the app should do, setting by setting

### How firm each claim is

| Strength | Claims |
|---|---|
| Well supported | Slow breathing near 6 breaths/min raises heart rate variability while it is performed. Breathing exercises give small-to-medium reductions in self-reported stress against mostly passive controls. Short breaks reduce fatigue and raise vigor. Movement raises heart rate as much as or more than stress does, and wrist sensors are least accurate during rapid heart rate changes. Interruptions cost errors and stress. |
| Weak or mixed | Any one technique (cyclic sighing, box, "coherent" 5.5/min) beating another. A longer exhale beating an equal one. Sessions shorter than 5 minutes. | Unproven or folklore | Detecting stress from heart rate alone, including +30 bpm for 15 s and 130 bpm. A drop in heart rate as proof the exercise worked. Breath holds or 4-7-8 adding benefit. Heart rate or HRV deciding when to break. 52/17, 90-minute ultradian blocks, 50/10 as an optimum, the 10-to-15-minute attention span, "23 minutes to refocus". Breathing breaks or any break schedule raising grades. |

Two consequences of these settings should be stated to the student inside the app. The rebuilt heart-rate nudge is likely to stay silent through much real study stress: in the one small wrist-tracker study of exams that we found (18 students), exam-time heart rate averaged under +10 bpm above baseline, which sits below any threshold that movement does not swamp. And when it does fire it still cannot tell tension from a walk to the kitchen, a phone call or a laugh, which is why it asks a question and offers "I was moving" as an answer. That answer could also serve as calibration data: logged beside the tense, tired or fine answers recorded at ordinary breaks, it could show whether heart-rate prompts land on tense moments more often than clock-timed ones do; the app does not yet make that comparison.

If only three changes are made, the order is clear. Adding the break timer brings in the component with the strongest evidence. Lengthening the sustain time to minutes, the wait to an hour and replacing the chime and auto-start with a silent question removes most false alarms and most of their cost. Switching the default to five minutes of 4-in/6-out breathing and replacing the heart-rate score with a self-rating aligns the exercise with what the trials tested and measured.

## What the student can realistically expect for grades and exams

This app can help a student feel better while studying. It has not been shown to raise anyone's grades.

The realistic gains are about comfort. In single-session studies, a few minutes of slow breathing tended to leave people feeling calmer for a short while, and in one study of 139 students the mood benefit was gone once the task was done ([Pilcher et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2505897)). Regular short breaks leave people less tired and more energetic by a modest amount, without reliably improving performance on demanding mental work ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)). Some small experiments find slightly fewer mistakes on attention and memory tests taken right after slow breathing, but one found nothing, the tests were laboratory puzzles and not schoolwork, and no pooled analysis turned up in our search ([Laborde et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1027/0269-8803/a000279); [Liang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1155134)).

For grades themselves the evidence is close to empty. We know of no randomized trial that tests breathing prompts or break timers against real exam grades. The one study we found linking a biofeedback relaxation program (with breathing training) to better university grades compared 152 students with 81 others and was not described as randomized ([Aritzeta et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-017-9367-z)). Deep breaths just before a timed math test improved scores in one study of 122 primary-school pupils, which has no direct replication that we could find ([Khng 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2016.1233095)), and yogic breathing before a final exam made no difference to anxiety in 119 nursing students ([Ortega-Donaire et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11040574)). Other popular pre-exam fixes have shrunk on closer inspection. Writing about worries shows a negligible effect across 21 studies ([O'Meara & Lovett 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/10615806.2025.2552857)). Telling yourself that nerves help has a small average benefit, d of 0.23 falling to 0.14 after correction for publication bias ([Bosshard & Gomez 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-58408-w)), and a 2026 replication across seven institutions found no effect ([Thormodsæter et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.25-04-0055)).

Nervous students do tend to score lower, but the cause is less clear. In one study of 309 medical students, anxiety no longer predicted the exam score once prior knowledge (practice-exam results) was controlled for, and anxious students had gained less knowledge during the preparation weeks ([Theobald et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221119391)). Among the ten learning techniques Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed, the two rated highest in utility are unglamorous: testing yourself and spreading study of the same material across days ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)), a rating based on learning across many tasks rather than on grades specifically. Sleeping long and regularly was associated with higher grades in an observational study of 88 students, while the single night before a test showed no relation to the score ([Okano et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-019-0055-z)). A fair expectation is that the app makes long study sessions more comfortable and easier to sustain, helps most for students who get tense, and leaves the grade to depend on what is done between the breaks.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the app's design. The part built as the centerpiece, a heart-rate alarm, is the part with no validation, and the part it lacks, short breaks, is the part with meta-analytic support for vigor and fatigue. The strap still earns its place, though in a different role. It is a weak detector, a serviceable reminder, and a good logging instrument, and the third role is the most interesting. As far as we know, nobody has trialed heart-rate-triggered breathing prompts in students while they study, so every student who uses the app is running that experiment on themselves. If the app records a one-tap rating at heart-rate prompts, at scheduled breaks and at self-started sessions, it produces the comparison the literature is missing, and it gives a principled reason to switch the heart-rate nudge off for any student in whom it proves no better than the clock.

Three questions would change these recommendations if answered. A head-to-head trial of one, two and five minutes would settle whether the quick option deserves to be the default. A preregistered replication of cyclic sighing would settle whether its mood advantage is real. And the notes behind this report did not verify whether the strap's broadcast can carry beat-to-beat intervals; most published stress-detection methods use beat-to-beat intervals or motion data, so their availability would reopen the detection question that heart rate alone has not answered.
